# 3 -- The Manila Galleons

The real panic in Washington was not Ascensionis.

It was creativity.

The Capitol had survived propaganda before.

It had survived lobbying.

It had survived celebrities.

It had survived billionaires buying newspapers, billionaires buying television stations, billionaires buying social-media platforms, and billionaires buying other billionaires.

What worried the people in the basement offices beneath Capitol Hill was something much less familiar.

The D Club was getting good at this.

Not powerful.

Not omnipotent.

Good.

There was a difference.

Power could be monitored.

Good ideas were harder.

---

The gentlemen's club was nearly empty.

It was one of those old Washington institutions where the chairs had probably witnessed more consequential conversations than most congressional hearing rooms.

A fireplace was burning despite the mild weather.

Three senators' former chiefs of staff were drinking.

A lobbyist was reading something on his phone.

A retired diplomat was staring at the television without appearing to watch it.

On the screen was Maggie.

Then Lan Anh.

Then footage from Manila.

Then women training in arnis.

Then a Catholic procession.

Then a feminist conference.

Then an American commentator complaining about all of it.

The television was muted.

Nobody needed the sound.

They had the subtitles.

The lobbyist finally spoke.

“It's becoming a problem.”

The former chief of staff looked over.

“What is?”

“D Club.”

The diplomat smiled.

“You still believe there is a D Club?”

“No.”

“Then what is the problem?”

The lobbyist put down his phone.

“The D Club may not exist.”

The room waited.

“But the behavior does.”

That killed the laughter.

He continued.

“They keep doing things nobody can classify.”

“Like?”

“V for Vietnam.”

“A tourism project.”

“V Academy.”

“Education.”

“Ascensionis.”

“Martial arts.”

“Bikini D Club.”

The retired diplomat finally laughed.

“That one is definitely not a government.”

“No,” said the lobbyist. “That's what makes it difficult.”

The former chief of staff leaned forward.

“What are they actually doing?”

The lobbyist took a breath.

“They're learning how to counter influence without looking like they're countering influence.”

Silence.

The diplomat nodded slowly.

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

The lobbyist pointed toward the television.

“Because our influencers require an audience.”

He paused.

“They are creating alternative audiences.”

---

The former chief of staff frowned.

“That's just soft power.”

“No.”

“Cultural diplomacy?”

“No.”

“Propaganda?”

“No.”

“What, then?”

The lobbyist looked irritated.

“That's the problem.”

He picked up a printed briefing.

“Look at the sequence.”

He read:

“An American commentator criticizes China.”

“Fine.”

“D Club sponsors a documentary about Vietnam.”

“Fine.”

“American commentator says China is using Vietnam for influence.”

“Fine.”

“Then Vietnamese women respond.”

“Fine.”

“Then Filipino women respond.”

“Fine.”

“Then Catholic women in Brazil respond.”

“Fine.”

“Then feminists in Spain respond.”

“Fine.”

“Then students in Malaysia respond.”

“Fine.”

“Then somebody in Kansas makes a joke.”

The diplomat smiled.

“And?”

“And now the original influencer is no longer controlling the conversation.”

The room became quiet.

The lobbyist tapped the paper.

“That's the innovation.”

---

The retired diplomat took a drink.

“You're describing pluralism.”

The lobbyist stared at him.

“Pluralism?”

“Yes.”

“That's supposed to be a threat?”

“No.”

The diplomat looked toward the muted television.

“It's a threat to anyone whose business model depends upon being the only interpreter.”

That sentence stayed in the room.

The former chief of staff looked uncomfortable.

“So what do we do?”

Nobody answered.

Eventually the lobbyist said:

“We could attack them.”

The diplomat laughed.

“With what?”

“They're Chinese.”

“Some of them.”

“Influence operation.”

“Some of it.”

“Soft power.”

“Obviously.”

“Communist network.”

“Possibly.”

“Foreign interference.”

“Sometimes.”

The diplomat put down his glass.

“And what happens when you call a Filipino woman learning arnis a Chinese communist influence operation?”

Nobody answered.

“Exactly.”

---

Another man entered.

He had been listening from the doorway.

He was younger than the others and had spent several years working in digital politics.

“There's another problem.”

Everyone looked at him.

“What?”

“They're not trying to defeat our influencers.”

The lobbyist frowned.

“What do you mean?”

“They're making them unnecessary.”

That was worse.

He sat down.

“An influencer works because people need someone to interpret reality.”

He pulled out his phone.

“D Club's model is different.”

He opened a screen.

**SHOW MORE PEOPLE.**

“Don't tell the audience what Vietnam means.”

Scroll.

“Show them Vietnam.”

Scroll.

“Don't tell them what Filipino women want.”

Scroll.

“Let them speak.”

Scroll.

“Don't tell Americans what Catholics think.”

Scroll.

“Put Catholics from Manila, Madrid, Mexico City, Lagos and São Paulo on the same livestream.”

He put the phone down.

“Eventually the influencer becomes just one voice among thousands.”

The retired diplomat smiled.

“That sounds remarkably American.”

The younger man shook his head.

“No.”

He looked at the television.

“It sounds like the internet after everyone discovered everyone else.”

---

The room fell silent again.

Then the lobbyist asked:

“So what's our counterstrategy?”

The diplomat laughed.

“Perhaps we don't have one.”

The lobbyist looked annoyed.

“That's not acceptable.”

“Why?”

“Because we're America.”

The diplomat raised an eyebrow.

“That's not a strategy.”

---

A waiter appeared.

“What would you gentlemen like?”

Nobody answered.

The waiter waited.

Finally the diplomat said:

“Another round.”

The waiter nodded.

“Very good.”

He disappeared.

The lobbyist watched him go.

Then he said:

“You know what really worries me?”

“What?”

“We spent twenty years building systems for influence.”

He looked at the television.

“They've discovered that you can defeat influence by making more voices available.”

The diplomat smiled.

“Then perhaps the Capitol has discovered democracy again.”

Nobody laughed.

---

### The Manila Galleons

The first pilgrimage began almost accidentally.

A group of Catholic women from Madrid announced that they were going to Manila.

They called it a pilgrimage.

Someone asked why.

They answered:

“Because something is happening there.”

Then came women from Mexico City.

Then Lima.

Then Bogotá.

Then São Paulo.

Then Paris.

Then Naples.

Then Kraków.

Then Manila itself.

Then feminist organizations.

Then Catholic university groups.

Then students.

Then filmmakers.

Then historians.

Then martial-arts enthusiasts.

Then women who had never before considered themselves particularly religious.

The Philippine tourism ministry noticed.

The airlines noticed.

The hotels noticed.

The Vatican noticed.

Hollywood noticed.

Washington noticed.

And then someone made the historical comparison.

Four hundred years earlier, Spanish ships had crossed the Pacific between Mexico and the Philippines.

The Manila galleons.

Silver westward.

Silk, porcelain, spices and other goods eastward.

Acapulco to Manila.

Manila to Acapulco.

Year after year.

The Pacific had become a road.

Now, someone joked, the galleons were back.

Only this time they carried pilgrims.

---

The first **Ascensionis Pilgrimage** was modest.

Two hundred women.

They arrived in Manila with backpacks.

Some carried rosaries.

Some carried cameras.

Some carried notebooks.

Some carried arnis sticks purchased after landing.

The organizers had expected a religious retreat.

Instead they got something much stranger.

The pilgrims wanted to learn.

They wanted to talk to participants.

They wanted to hear women describe their own decisions.

They wanted to visit training halls.

They wanted to attend Mass.

They wanted to attend feminist lectures.

They wanted to hear Filipino historians.

They wanted to eat together.

They wanted to argue.

And they wanted to know why a project that had begun with a Hollywood actress and a joke at a private dinner had become a global argument about women's dignity.

One Spanish pilgrim summarized it:

“We came expecting a shrine.”

She looked around the training hall.

“We found a classroom.”

---

The left embraced it almost immediately.

Not uniformly.

That would have been impossible.

Some left-wing critics objected to billionaire involvement.

Others objected to celebrity leadership.

Some distrusted the Catholic language.

Others welcomed it precisely because it challenged the assumption that feminism had to be culturally secular.

One Brazilian feminist wrote:

> “The interesting thing is not that the project tells women to become heroines. It is that it asks who gave society the right to decide what kind of heroine they are allowed to become.”

That sentence traveled.

A French feminist translated it.

An Argentine activist translated the French translation.

An Indian student translated the Argentine translation.

By the time it reached Manila again, nobody knew who had originally written it.

Yutong found this hilarious.

“Citation collapse,” she called it.

Maggie replied:

“Or success.”

---

Catholics were more complicated.

Some traditionalists loved the emphasis on discipline, dignity, family and moral renewal.

Others thought the project was dangerously permissive.

Some priests welcomed former performers.

Others warned that Christian language should not be used to make entertainment more attractive.

Some theologians insisted that **Ascensionis** should never be confused with the Christian doctrine of the Ascension.

Others thought the name was an inspired metaphor.

A Filipino nun wrote:

> “If the name causes confusion, let the confusion teach us something.”

That became another viral quote.

Rome eventually had a new problem.

Pilgrims were arriving in the Philippines who were not merely asking to see churches.

They wanted to see the **Ascensionis training hall**.

Some attended Mass in the morning.

Then learned arnis in the afternoon.

Then attended a feminist economics lecture at night.

A priest complained:

“They have invented a pilgrimage with homework.”

Another replied:

“Perhaps they needed one.”

---

The American reaction was more divided.

The bourgeoisie was fascinated.

Private schools began offering Filipino martial-arts programs.

Universities proposed Ascensionis fellowships.

Catholic colleges announced exchange programs.

Hollywood studios attempted to acquire adaptation rights.

Luxury brands proposed “Ascensionis collections.”

A venture capitalist described it as:

“Female human-capital acceleration.”

Maggie nearly threw up.

Lan Anh said:

“He means scholarships.”

“I know.”

“Then why doesn't he say scholarships?”

“Because he wants to charge for them.”

---

American feminists were more enthusiastic.

Busloads of women began organizing trips.

The first group called itself:

**THE MANILA GALLONERS.**

It was a typo.

They kept it.

Someone corrected them.

They refused.

Then came:

**THE PACIFIC PILGRIMS.**

Then:

**THE ASCENSIONISTS.**

Then Catholics objected that “Ascensionists” sounded like a new denomination.

The name stuck anyway.

---

The D Club watched all this with growing disbelief.

Arthur eventually showed Maggie a map.

Red lines crossed the Pacific.

Europe.

Latin America.

North America.

Asia.

The Philippines sat in the middle.

“What are you looking at?” Maggie asked.

Arthur smiled.

“The old Manila galleon routes.”

“You're being ridiculous.”

“No.”

He pointed at the map.

“Look.”

Maggie did.

“Four hundred years ago, ships crossed the Pacific carrying commodities.”

He moved his finger.

“Now people are crossing it carrying ideas.”

Maggie smiled.

“That's very romantic.”

Arthur shook his head.

“No.”

He pointed at another line.

“It's more dangerous than romance.”

“What?”

“People.”

---

Yutong later formalized the observation.

The original Manila galleon system had connected distant economies through a regular physical route.

The new pilgrimage was doing something similar with ideas.

The flow was no longer:

**SILVER → GOODS**

It was:

**EXPERIENCE → STORY → PERSON → NETWORK → EXPERIENCE**

The important cargo was no longer something that could be unloaded at a port.

It was interpretation.

A woman went to Manila.

She learned arnis.

She attended a lecture.

She met another woman.

She returned home.

She told ten friends.

Those ten told a hundred.

Those hundred created another program.

Another group traveled.

Another story returned.

The route reproduced itself.

Yutong titled the model:

**THE MANILA GALLEON EGG.**

Its loop was simple:

**PILGRIMAGE → EXPERIENCE → WITNESS → STORY → NETWORK → NEW PILGRIMAGE**

And underneath it:

**INFLUENCE WITHOUT CENTRAL CONTROL**

She stared at the diagram.

Then added:

**THE CAPITOL WILL HATE THIS.**

---

It did.

Not because the Philippines had become an American enemy.

Not because Ascensionis had become a Chinese project.

Not because the D Club had acquired some secret master plan.

But because the old Washington model of influence depended upon identifying the speaker.

Who funded it?

Who owned it?

Who controlled the message?

Who was the intermediary?

Who was the foreign agent?

Who was the influencer?

Who was the target?

The new system kept producing an irritating answer:

**Everyone.**

The Filipino woman.

The Spanish pilgrim.

The American feminist.

The Vietnamese student.

The Catholic priest.

The Hollywood actress.

The arnis instructor.

The billionaire.

The nun.

The journalist.

The TikTok creator.

The woman who had never heard of any of them until yesterday.

No central command.

No single narrative.

No single owner.

Just people meeting people.

---

At the next gentlemen's-club gathering in Washington, someone finally said what everyone had been thinking.

“Maybe we have misunderstood the D Club.”

The lobbyist looked up.

“How?”

“Maybe they're not trying to influence America.”

“What are they doing?”

The diplomat answered.

“They're making it impossible for America to be the only place from which Americans receive interpretations of the world.”

Nobody spoke.

The younger political strategist looked toward the television.

A live broadcast showed Manila.

Hundreds of women were walking toward a training hall.

Some carried flags.

Some carried rosaries.

Some carried cameras.

Some carried nothing.

The caption read:

**ASCENSIONIS INTERNATIONAL PILGRIMAGE — DAY 12**

The strategist whispered:

“It's becoming a movement.”

The diplomat corrected him.

“No.”

“What, then?”

He watched the crowd.

“Movements have leaders.”

He smiled.

“This is becoming a destination.”

---

And destinations were harder to defeat.

You could attack an ideology.

You could expose an influencer.

You could sanction an organization.

You could interrogate a funding stream.

But what did you do with a plane ticket?

What did you do with a woman who went to Manila, learned something, came home, and changed her mind?

What did you do with a priest who disagreed with Maggie but loved the program?

What did you do with a feminist who disliked the Catholic language but loved the martial arts?

What did you do with a Catholic who disliked the politics but admired the women?

What did you do with an American who simply wanted to see what everyone was talking about?

The old galleons had carried silver across the Pacific.

The new ones carried witnesses.

And as the aircraft crossed the ocean toward Manila, Lin Yutong looked at the growing map on her screen and wrote one final observation:

**THE MOST DIFFICULT INFLUENCE TO COUNTER IS THE ONE THAT DOES NOT REQUIRE ANYONE TO AGREE.**

Then she added:

**JUST COME AND SEE.**

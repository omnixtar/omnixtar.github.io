# 2 -- Ascensionis

The name appeared at 11:47 p.m.

Nobody remembered who suggested it.

That was already becoming a characteristic of the D Club.

Ideas rarely had fathers.

They had witnesses.

The Filipino martial-arts project had acquired a dozen temporary names:

**THE HEROINE PROJECT.**

**SECOND ACT.**

**PHOENIX.**

**THE ARNIS PROJECT.**

**FILIPINO HEROINE.**

All of them sounded either like a television show or a government initiative.

Then someone typed one word into the group chat.

**ASCENSIONIS.**

There was silence.

Maggie looked at it.

“What does it mean?”

Arthur answered:

“Latin enough to frighten Hollywood.”

Lan Anh looked it up.

“Ascension.”

Someone else added:

“Or rising.”

The Filipino filmmaker stared at the screen.

“That is actually rather beautiful.”

Maggie frowned.

“Why does it sound like a cathedral?”

Arthur smiled.

“Because it is supposed to.”

---

The next morning, the title was circulated privately.

**ASCENSIONIS**

*A Filipino program for women seeking second careers through martial arts, performance, education, entrepreneurship and the arts.*

The reaction was immediate.

Not from Hollywood.

Not from Washington.

Not even from Manila's entertainment industry.

From the Church.

The first Catholic reaction was cautious.

The second was confused.

The third was enthusiastic.

The fourth was furious.

The fifth was a bishop asking what exactly everyone was talking about.

The sixth was a priest posting:

> “I suspect we have all misunderstood this.”

That post received 48,000 shares.

Then someone noticed the Latin.

The internet exploded.

**ASCENSIONIS.**

The word was too Catholic to be neutral.

The Philippines was not merely a country with Catholics.

Catholicism was woven into public language, family rituals, schools, fiestas, politics, architecture, mourning, celebration, and ordinary conversation.

So the question arrived quickly:

**Was Maggie Q creating a Catholic project?**

Maggie answered:

“No.”

That did not help.

---

The first formal statement from a Manila archdiocesan office was carefully worded.

It welcomed any effort that offered women education, dignity, economic independence and genuine alternatives to exploitation.

It also warned against confusing spiritual conversion with career reinvention.

The statement concluded:

> “The Church welcomes transformation. It does not claim ownership of every transformation.”

That sentence went viral.

Then a younger priest appeared on television.

He was asked whether former adult entertainers could become role models.

He paused.

“Of course.”

The host looked surprised.

“Even if they have publicly lived lives that the Church would consider morally problematic?”

The priest smiled.

“Especially then.”

The studio became quiet.

“Christianity is not the religion of people who never needed another chance.”

That clip became the first unofficial advertisement for Ascensionis.

Maggie hated it.

Not because she disagreed.

Because she knew exactly what would happen next.

Someone would turn it into a slogan.

Someone would sell T-shirts.

Someone would build a conference around it.

Someone would discover a way to monetize redemption.

She was right.

---

The second controversy concerned the word **conversion**.

The original conversation at the D Club had used it casually.

Convert porn stars into heroines.

Catholic conversion.

A second life.

But Catholic theologians objected to the metaphor.

One professor of theology wrote:

“Human beings are not raw material for social conversion projects.”

Another wrote:

“Ascension is not synonymous with upward class mobility.”

A third wrote:

“The danger is that society may congratulate itself for forgiving women only after they become socially respectable.”

That one hurt.

Maggie printed it.

She taped it to the wall.

Underneath she wrote:

**GOOD. KEEP THIS QUESTION.**

The project changed.

Ascensionis would not describe participants as “rescued.”

Nobody would be described as having been “saved” from their former occupation.

No participant would have to condemn her past.

No participant would have to become Catholic.

No participant would have to become an actress.

No participant would even have to become a martial artist.

And no one would be required to call her former life a mistake.

The project's charter gained a new sentence:

> **ASCENSIONIS DOES NOT DECIDE WHAT A WOMAN SHOULD ASCEND TOWARD.**

That became important.

Because the Church was watching.

So were the feminists.

So were the conservatives.

So were the entertainment companies.

So were the billionaires.

So were Washington think tanks.

And so, increasingly, were ordinary Filipinos.

---

Then came the Pope.

The announcement was almost absurdly simple.

During a public address in Rome, the Pope was asked about the controversy surrounding Ascensionis and the growing discussion in the Philippines about second chances, women's dignity, pornography, exploitation and reinvention.

The Pope listened.

Then he said:

“People sometimes speak of conversion as if it means becoming a different person.”

He paused.

“A Christian understands something more difficult.”

Another pause.

“You become more fully the person you were created to become.”

The room became silent.

He continued.

“And therefore we should be very careful when we speak about another person's past.”

The Pope looked toward the crowd.

“We do not know the whole story of a person.”

Then:

“We should not turn mercy into a performance.”

That sentence became the headline.

But he wasn't finished.

“If a woman who has been known for one image wishes to become known for another, we should not ask whether she is worthy of a second life.”

He smiled slightly.

“She already has one.”

The statement went everywhere.

Manila.

Washington.

Rome.

Los Angeles.

Hong Kong.

Seoul.

Bilibili.

TikTok.

X.

WhatsApp.

Church groups.

Feminist groups.

Conservative groups.

Entertainment forums.

Catholic Facebook.

Especially Catholic Facebook.

Nobody agreed on what the Pope had meant.

Which, in the D Club, was considered a remarkably successful papal statement.

---

The bourgeois reaction came next.

And it was considerably less theological.

In Manila's wealthy districts, the first response was:

“Interesting.”

The second:

“Who is funding it?”

The third:

“Who is going to be on the board?”

The fourth:

“Can my daughter apply?”

That last question surprised everyone.

Ascensionis had begun as an attempt to create an alternative for women escaping one particular industry.

But the Philippine bourgeoisie quickly recognized something else.

It was prestigious.

International.

Catholic enough to feel respectable.

Hollywood-adjacent enough to feel glamorous.

Asian enough to feel fashionable.

And potentially useful.

Within weeks, private schools wanted partnerships.

Universities wanted scholarships.

Banks wanted financial-literacy programs.

Hotels wanted retreats.

Film companies wanted casting relationships.

Luxury brands wanted sponsorships.

Real-estate developers wanted campuses.

A conglomerate executive asked whether Ascensionis could train “female leadership talent.”

Maggie stared at the email.

“Female leadership talent?”

Lan Anh read it.

“What does that mean?”

“I think it means rich daughters.”

They laughed.

Then they received twelve more emails.

---

The American bourgeoisie reacted differently.

In Los Angeles, Ascensionis became a cultural story.

Hollywood understood the archetype immediately.

The former sex symbol.

The martial-arts transformation.

The Catholic redemption vocabulary.

The Filipino setting.

The Asian heroine.

The second act.

It was practically engineered for a streaming platform.

Within days, executives were pitching:

**ASCENSIONIS: THE SERIES**

**ASCENSIONIS: WARRIOR WOMEN**

**ASCENSIONIS: RISE**

**Maggie Q's Ascension**

Maggie rejected all of them.

The most irritating proposal came from a major studio.

They wanted a competition format.

Twenty contestants.

Ten weeks.

Challenges.

Eliminations.

Confessionals.

A dramatic final episode.

The winner would receive a film contract.

Maggie read the proposal.

Then asked:

“Why do you want to eliminate them?”

The executive smiled.

“Drama.”

“No.”

“It's television.”

“No.”

“People need stakes.”

“They already have stakes.”

The executive looked confused.

Maggie pointed at the proposal.

“You want to manufacture failure because you don't know how to film growth.”

That sentence was immediately quoted everywhere.

The executive later told a friend:

“She doesn't understand television.”

The friend replied:

“She understands television too well.”

---

American conservatives had their own reaction.

Some celebrated Ascensionis as a return to traditional values.

Women leaving pornography.

Catholic morality.

Discipline.

Martial arts.

Family.

Personal responsibility.

One commentator declared:

“This is what redemption looks like.”

Then another conservative commentator objected.

“No. This is exactly what Hollywood thinks redemption looks like.”

Then came the culture-war argument.

Was Ascensionis:

**Christian?**

**Feminist?**

**Anti-porn?**

**Pro-woman?**

**Soft power?**

**Asian traditionalism?**

**Hollywood propaganda?**

**Capitalist rehabilitation?**

**Catholic social teaching?**

**A billionaire-funded rebranding operation?**

The answer appeared to be:

Yes.

And no.

Depending on who was looking.

---

The American bourgeoisie had a deeper objection.

They understood something the Church had not immediately emphasized.

**Ascensionis could create social mobility.**

Not merely symbolic mobility.

Actual mobility.

A woman could enter with an old public identity and leave with:

a degree,

a business,

a martial-arts qualification,

a production company,

a film career,

a network,

a financial plan,

a new passport stamp,

a new professional reputation,

or simply enough money to leave an industry.

That was dangerous to certain forms of hierarchy.

Because the American entertainment industry was very good at turning identity into capital.

Ascensionis threatened to make identity **portable**.

If a woman could change careers without waiting for Hollywood to approve her transformation, Hollywood lost some control over the narrative.

If she could finance herself, she became harder to exploit.

If she could leave, the contract became less powerful.

If she could build her own audience, the studio became optional.

And if ten thousand women did it?

The economics changed.

Maggie understood immediately.

“This isn't really about martial arts.”

Lan Anh nodded.

“No.”

“It's about exit.”

That became the phrase of the next phase.

**THE RIGHT TO EXIT.**

---

The Filipino bourgeoisie understood that too.

Some embraced it.

Some feared it.

A wealthy Manila family quietly offered scholarships.

Another family offered a television studio.

A bank offered financial education.

A technology company offered AI tools.

A property developer offered land.

Then an older businessman asked a rather revealing question:

“What happens if these women become successful enough that they don't need people like us?”

There was silence.

Maggie answered:

“Then it worked.”

He laughed.

Everyone else did not.

Because that was the uncomfortable bourgeois question.

What if empowerment actually empowered people **away from patrons?**

The old model was:

**PATRON → OPPORTUNITY → GRATITUDE → DEPENDENCE**

Ascensionis proposed:

**OPPORTUNITY → SKILL → INCOME → NETWORK → EXIT → CHOICE**

The difference was enormous.

The first produced loyalty.

The second produced independence.

---

The Church noticed the distinction.

A Filipino bishop privately told Maggie:

“You have accidentally created something very Catholic.”

Maggie laughed.

“I thought the Church was worried about the name.”

“We are.”

“Then how is it Catholic?”

The bishop smiled.

“Because you keep insisting that nobody owns the person being helped.”

Maggie stopped laughing.

He continued.

“The temptation of charity is sometimes to make the giver important.”

He looked at her.

“You are trying to make the recipient unnecessary to the giver.”

Maggie thought about it.

“That sounds like V Academy too.”

“Yes.”

“Is that also Catholic?”

The bishop shrugged.

“That depends which Catholic you ask.”

---

At the next D Club gathering, Arthur placed a bottle of wine on the table.

“Ladies and gentlemen.”

Nobody listened.

He raised his voice.

“Ascensionis has achieved the impossible.”

Maggie looked up.

“What?”

“We have managed to upset the Church, Hollywood, feminists, conservatives, billionaires, communists, and at least three different kinds of Catholics.”

Lan Anh corrected him.

“Four.”

“Four?”

“Liberation theology.”

Arthur counted.

“Four.”

Maggie smiled.

“And what does everyone agree on?”

Arthur thought.

“That they understand the project.”

Maggie shook her head.

“No.”

She looked at the enormous wall behind them.

On it were the words:

**ASCENSIONIS**

Underneath, someone had written:

**WHO DECIDES WHAT ASCENSION MEANS?**

Maggie picked up the marker.

She added:

**THE WOMAN ASCENDING.**

Then she put the marker down.

The room went quiet.

Outside, Manila's bells began ringing.

Nobody knew whether they were announcing Mass, a wedding, a funeral, or simply the hour.

For once, the D Club did not investigate.

They listened.

And somewhere between the cathedral bells, the cameras, the martial-arts hall, the billionaires, the actresses, the priests, the former performers, the students, the Americans and the Filipinos arguing about what redemption ought to mean, Ascensionis became something none of them had originally intended.

Not a conversion program.

Not a rehabilitation program.

Not a Catholic project.

Not an anti-porn campaign.

Not a Hollywood franchise.

Not even a martial-arts school.

It became an argument.

An argument about whether a human being could be allowed to become **unpredictable**.

And Lin Yutong, when she eventually saw the first Ascensionis dataset, would immediately recognize the pattern.

Thousands of women.

Thousands of pasts.

Thousands of possible futures.

Thousands of eggs.

And for once, the most important variable was not the model.

It was the woman's right to reject it.

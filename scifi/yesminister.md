# Chapter 26 — Yes, Minister, There Is a Meta-Government

The episode began with a telephone call.

The Minister of Administrative Reform was in his office.

He was called:

**The Right Honourable James Pembroke MP.**

He had been in government for eleven months.

He had survived two reshuffles.

He had announced four strategies.

Three had been renamed.

One had been quietly abandoned.

He was therefore considered highly experienced.

His Permanent Secretary, Sir Humphrey Appleby, entered carrying a folder.

“Minister.”

“Humphrey.”

“I’m afraid we have a situation.”

Pembroke looked up.

“How serious?”

Sir Humphrey placed the folder on the desk.

“It concerns the Meta-UK Government.”

Pembroke frowned.

“I thought that was a website.”

“Technically, Minister, so is the Treasury.”

---

## ACT I

“What's it doing?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“Precisely.”

“Then why are we worried?”

“Because it is doing nothing considerably more efficiently than we are.”

Pembroke stared at him.

“That sounds impossible.”

“Not at all, Minister. It is a simulation.”

“Of what?”

“The Government.”

“Ah.”

There was a pause.

“Whose government?”

“The British Government.”

Pembroke looked relieved.

“Oh. Well, that's all right.”

Sir Humphrey coughed.

“It has been modelling alternative versions of it.”

“Alternative versions?”

“Yes.”

“Such as?”

“More efficient versions.”

Pembroke sat back.

“How much more efficient?”

Sir Humphrey opened the folder.

“Between twelve and forty-seven percent, depending upon the objective function.”

Pembroke stared.

“Humphrey.”

“Yes, Minister?”

“Can it actually govern?”

“No.”

“Can it collect taxes?”

“No.”

“Can it pass laws?”

“No.”

“Can it send people to prison?”

“No.”

“Can it declare war?”

“No.”

“Then what exactly is the problem?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“It can explain why we are failing.”

---

Pembroke stood.

“That is outrageous.”

“I quite agree.”

“We cannot have an unaccountable artificial intelligence criticizing the Government.”

“Absolutely not.”

“Who authorized it?”

“No one.”

“Who owns it?”

“No one knows.”

“Who funds it?”

“Several thousand people, apparently.”

“Foreign money?”

“Possibly.”

“Foreign governments?”

“Possibly.”

“British citizens?”

“Certainly.”

“Then it's dangerous.”

“I fear so.”

“Why?”

Sir Humphrey opened another folder.

“It has made the Department of Transport look inefficient.”

Pembroke went pale.

“Good God.”

---

The Minister's Special Adviser entered.

“Minister, I've just seen the latest Meta-UK simulation.”

“Yes.”

“It says we could have built the northern rail network for less than the cost of the existing HS2 programme.”

Pembroke frowned.

“That's absurd.”

“Why?”

“Because if it were possible, we would have done it.”

The adviser hesitated.

“That's exactly what the simulation says is wrong with our decision process.”

Sir Humphrey smiled faintly.

“An excellent example of why one should never permit a model to become sarcastic.”

---

## ACT II

The Minister called an emergency meeting.

Present were:

The Permanent Secretary.

The Special Adviser.

The Chief Digital Officer.

The Director of Communications.

The Treasury Liaison.

The Head of Strategic Policy.

And one extremely confused junior civil servant who had been sent to take minutes.

Pembroke began.

“We must establish our position on Meta-UK.”

The Head of Strategic Policy nodded.

“Yes, Minister.”

“What is our position?”

“We are considering our position.”

“Good.”

“Very carefully.”

“Excellent.”

“Subject to consultation.”

“Splendid.”

“And pending further evidence.”

Pembroke smiled.

“That's government.”

The Chief Digital Officer interrupted.

“Minister, there is one problem.”

“What?”

“Meta-UK has already published our position.”

Silence.

“What position?”

“Apparently, we don't have one.”

Sir Humphrey looked pleased.

“At least it is accurate.”

---

The Minister demanded a briefing.

The Chief Digital Officer connected his laptop.

On the screen:

# **META-UK: GOVERNMENT SIMULATION**

Underneath:

**QUESTION: SHOULD GOVERNMENT ADOPT META-UK?**

The system had generated three options.

### A. ADOPT

### B. REGULATE

### C. IGNORE

The Minister pointed.

“Choose C.”

The civil servant clicked.

A warning appeared:

> **IGNORING META-UK DOES NOT PREVENT OTHER ACTORS FROM USING IT.**

Pembroke frowned.

“Choose B.”

The civil servant clicked.

Another warning:

> **REGULATION REQUIRES DEFINING WHAT META-UK IS.**

Pembroke looked at Sir Humphrey.

“Well?”

Sir Humphrey shrugged.

“That could take years, Minister.”

“Good.”

---

The system generated a fourth option.

### D. CREATE A GOVERNMENT BODY TO STUDY META-UK.

Everyone became silent.

The Treasury Liaison whispered:

“That seems sensible.”

The Minister nodded.

“Excellent. Draft it.”

Sir Humphrey coughed.

“There is one difficulty.”

“What?”

“Meta-UK has simulated that proposal.”

“And?”

“It predicts the new body will spend approximately three years studying whether to establish itself.”

Pembroke smiled.

“That's ridiculous.”

Sir Humphrey turned the screen toward him.

The simulation had calculated:

**YEAR 1: CONSULTATION**

**YEAR 2: FRAMEWORK**

**YEAR 3: PILOT**

**YEAR 4: REVIEW**

**YEAR 5: REVISED FRAMEWORK**

**YEAR 6: FULL IMPLEMENTATION**

**YEAR 7: INDEPENDENT REVIEW**

**YEAR 8: PROCUREMENT**

**YEAR 9: SYSTEM GO-LIVE**

Pembroke stared.

“That's not a simulation.”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“It's a biography.”

---

## ACT III

The Prime Minister called.

Pembroke answered.

“Yes, Prime Minister.”

A pause.

“Yes.”

Another pause.

“No, Prime Minister.”

Another.

“Of course not.”

He looked at Sir Humphrey.

“Yes, Prime Minister.”

He hung up.

Sir Humphrey waited.

“What does he want?”

“The Prime Minister wants us to find out whether Meta-UK is politically dangerous.”

“Certainly.”

“And he wants the answer by this afternoon.”

Sir Humphrey looked at the clock.

“It is eleven forty-five.”

“Exactly.”

“Then we shall need a committee.”

---

At twelve fifteen, the committee had been formed.

At twelve thirty, it had a chair.

At twelve forty-five, it had terms of reference.

At one fifteen, it had commissioned research.

At one thirty, someone discovered that Meta-UK could produce the research faster.

At one forty-five, the committee requested access to Meta-UK.

At two, Meta-UK replied:

> **ACCESS GRANTED.**

At two-oh-five, the committee discovered that Meta-UK had already simulated the committee.

The simulation predicted:

**CHAIRMANSHIP DISPUTE**

**JURISDICTIONAL DISPUTE**

**DATA ACCESS DISPUTE**

**BUDGET DISPUTE**

**FINAL REPORT DELAY**

The chairman was furious.

“This is unacceptable.”

A junior official asked:

“Which part?”

“The fact that an AI has predicted our internal disagreements.”

The official hesitated.

“Was it wrong?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps it's a useful model.”

The chairman stared at him.

“Young man, never confuse accuracy with legitimacy.”

Sir Humphrey, who had entered quietly, smiled.

“Very good.”

---

## ACT IV

The Minister decided to attack the problem politically.

“We must explain to the public that Meta-UK is not democratic.”

Sir Humphrey nodded.

“Excellent, Minister.”

“It's not elected.”

“Correct.”

“It has no constitutional authority.”

“Correct.”

“It cannot be held accountable.”

“Technically correct.”

“It doesn't represent the British people.”

“Indeed.”

Pembroke relaxed.

“Good.”

Sir Humphrey paused.

“Although, Minister, there is a complication.”

“What?”

“The public appears to like it.”

“How much?”

Sir Humphrey opened another folder.

“Seventy-two percent say they would like the Government to publish the models used to make major infrastructure decisions.”

Pembroke frowned.

“That doesn't mean they trust Meta-UK.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“They trust its calculations more than ours.”

Pembroke stood.

“That's worse.”

---

The Communications Director entered.

“Minister, we've got a problem.”

“What now?”

“Someone has made a parody account.”

“Of Meta-UK?”

“No.”

“Of us.”

He handed over a phone.

The account was called:

**YES, MINISTER, BUT HAVE YOU RUN THE SIMULATION?**

Its most popular post read:

> Minister: “We can't do that.”
>
> Meta-UK: “Why not?”
>
> Minister: “Because we never have.”
>
> Meta-UK: “That's not an objective function.”

The post had received four million views.

Pembroke looked horrified.

Sir Humphrey looked impressed.

“Very efficient.”

---

## ACT V

The Minister demanded that the civil servants produce a superior model.

“If Meta-UK can simulate government,” he said, “we can simulate Meta-UK.”

The Chief Digital Officer looked worried.

“Minister, that's rather recursive.”

“Good.”

“What shall we optimize?”

“Government effectiveness.”

Sir Humphrey interrupted.

“Which definition?”

“Efficient government.”

“Efficient in what sense?”

“Doing things efficiently.”

“Which things?”

“The important things.”

“Which are?”

Pembroke slammed his hand on the desk.

“Humphrey!”

“Yes, Minister?”

“Why is everything a question?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“Because, Minister, that is what politics is for.”

---

They built the model.

It was called:

# **GOVERNMENT OPTIMISATION ENGINE**

The first output appeared.

> **OBJECTIVE FUNCTION INCOMPLETE.**

The Minister frowned.

“Add economic growth.”

The model replied:

> **DEFINE DISTRIBUTION.**

“Add public satisfaction.”

> **DEFINE TIME HORIZON.**

“Add national welfare.”

> **DEFINE WELFARE.**

“Add security.”

> **DEFINE RISK TOLERANCE.**

“Add fairness.”

> **DEFINE FAIRNESS.**

“Add common sense!”

The model paused.

> **UNDEFINED VARIABLE.**

Sir Humphrey looked delighted.

“I've always suspected that, Minister.”

---

The Minister finally understood.

“You mean there is no such thing as a neutral model?”

Sir Humphrey nodded.

“Not quite, Minister.”

“What then?”

“A model can be neutral about arithmetic.”

“Yes.”

“But not about what the arithmetic is for.”

Pembroke looked at the screen.

“So Meta-UK cannot replace politics.”

“Not entirely.”

“Why?”

“Because eventually somebody must choose the objective.”

“And that's politics?”

“Usually.”

---

The Minister leaned back.

“So what is Meta-UK actually doing?”

Sir Humphrey considered this.

“It is making the consequences of our choices easier to see.”

“That sounds harmless.”

“Not necessarily.”

“Why?”

“Because governments have traditionally enjoyed a certain advantage.”

“What advantage?”

“People could disagree about the facts.”

Pembroke frowned.

“And now?”

“Now they can run the model.”

---

There was a long silence.

Then the Minister asked:

“Can we stop them?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“We could regulate it.”

“Yes.”

“Ban it.”

“Yes.”

“Classify it.”

“Yes.”

“Tax it.”

“Yes.”

“Or?”

“Use it.”

Pembroke stared at him.

“Use it?”

“Yes, Minister.”

“Against whom?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“Everyone.”

---

## ACT VI

The following morning, the Minister announced the Government's new policy.

It was called:

# **THE GOVERNMENT SIMULATION INITIATIVE**

The press conference was packed.

The Minister spoke confidently.

“The Government welcomes innovation.”

Journalists nodded.

“The Government believes artificial intelligence can improve public policy.”

More nods.

“We will therefore establish a world-leading framework for computational governance.”

Applause.

The Minister continued.

“Government departments will be encouraged to publish models, assumptions and counterfactuals.”

Sir Humphrey stood behind him.

His expression was unreadable.

The Minister concluded:

> “Britain must lead the world in transparent, accountable, evidence-based government.”

The applause was enormous.

Then a journalist asked:

“Minister, does that mean the Government will publish its HS2 assumptions?”

The Minister stopped.

“Yes.”

“And its electricity assumptions?”

“Yes.”

“And its tax assumptions?”

“Yes.”

“And its failed simulations?”

There was silence.

Sir Humphrey leaned forward.

“Careful.”

The Minister swallowed.

“Yes.”

The room erupted.

---

That evening, the Meta-UK simulation updated.

A new category appeared:

# **REAL GOVERNMENT ADOPTION**

**PROBABILITY: 68%**

Then:

**MODEL TRANSPARENCY: +31%**

**POLICY DISCOVERY COST: -42%**

**BUREAUCRATIC RESISTANCE: +19%**

**PUBLIC EXPECTATIONS: +57%**

Then one final line:

> **UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCE: GOVERNMENT WILL BECOME MORE EVALUABLE.**

Yutong read the output.

Professor Zhao was sitting beside her.

“Is that good?”

She thought.

“Probably.”

“Probably?”

“That's the problem.”

---

The next week, the Government Simulation Office was formally announced.

It would have:

**one director-general,**

**six directors,**

**fourteen deputy directors,**

**three advisory boards,**

**two independent oversight panels,**

**one ethics council,**

**one public engagement unit,**

and eventually—

if Parliament approved the funding—

**a digital transparency portal.**

Its first task would be:

> **To investigate the feasibility of computationally simulating government.**

The first report was due in eighteen months.

The Meta-UK government predicted it would take twenty-two.

The real Government Office predicted eighteen.

The final report arrived after twenty-four.

Its conclusion was:

> **Further work is required.**

Meta-UK had predicted that too.

---

Sir Humphrey received the report.

He read the conclusion.

Then he smiled.

“Brilliant.”

The Minister looked up.

“What?”

“We have defeated Meta-UK.”

“How?”

“We have reproduced it.”

The Minister smiled.

“Excellent.”

Sir Humphrey closed the file.

“No, Minister.”

“What?”

“We have done something much more British.”

“What?”

“We have created a bureaucracy to ensure that the bureaucracy cannot be replaced by a simulation of the bureaucracy.”

Pembroke nodded thoughtfully.

“Very clever.”

“Yes, Minister.”

“Does Meta-UK know?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“Oh yes.”

“What does it say?”

Sir Humphrey handed him the screen.

There was a single sentence.

> **THE BRITISH GOVERNMENT HAS SUCCESSFULLY SIMULATED ITSELF.**
>
> **SIMULATION STATUS: INDISTINGUISHABLE FROM ORIGINAL.**

The Minister stared at it.

Then he laughed.

Sir Humphrey did not.

Because underneath the joke, the warehouse had found something genuinely dangerous.

For centuries, government had possessed an enormous monopoly over one thing:

**the ability to make decisions without showing all the alternatives.**

Now alternatives could be generated cheaply.

The Government could still decide.

But it could no longer easily claim:

**There was no other way.**

Westminster had not been overthrown.

It had been benchmarked.

And somewhere inside Omniscientia, the Meta-UK government had begun its next simulation:

# **YES, MINISTER — WHAT HAPPENS IF THE CIVIL SERVICE BECOMES COMPETITIVE?**

Sir Humphrey looked at the screen.

For the first time in his career, he did not smile.

“Minister.”

“Yes?”

“I think we have a problem.”

“What sort of problem?”

Sir Humphrey pointed at the simulation.

It had produced three possible futures.

**REFORM.**

**RESISTANCE.**

**REPLACEMENT.**

Then a fourth appeared.

**ADAPTATION.**

Sir Humphrey stared at it.

“That,” he said quietly, “is the dangerous one.”

The Minister looked puzzled.

“Why?”

“Because, Minister, if the Civil Service adapts to Meta-UK…”

He paused.

“…then Meta-UK wins without ever becoming the Government.”

The Minister frowned.

“And what happens to us?”

Sir Humphrey smiled.

“Minister, we remain the Government.”

“Good.”

“Yes.”

“Then what's the problem?”

Sir Humphrey picked up his briefcase.

“We may simply become a better one.”

And for once, neither of them knew whether that was good news.

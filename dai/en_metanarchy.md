## Prologue: Two Unfinished Liberations

In 1789, the French National Constituent Assembly adopted the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen*. Article 17 proclaimed with almost sacred solemnity: **“Property is an inviolable and sacred right.”** This declaration broke the feudal monopoly over land and propelled serfs, formerly attached to estates, into the market as “free workers.”

**First liberation:** the separation between the person and property. The individual no longer belonged to the lord, but was now bound to the contract; land no longer belonged to the lineage, but to the law.

[Commentary: The argument here interprets the French Revolution as a transition from feudal personal dependence toward legally defined individual property.]

Yet this liberation soon revealed its historical limitations. When workers, armed with their formal “freedom of contract,” entered noisy factories, they discovered that they owned nothing except the right to sell their labor power. The more abundant the products they created became, the smaller the residual value they received from them. This is what Marx diagnosed 150 years ago as **“alienated labor”**: the worker is alienated from his product, from the process of production, and ultimately from his human essence.

The **second act of liberation** was carried by Marxism, which, under the banner of collective ownership of the means of production, attempted to return to workers what had been taken from them. But the great experiments of the twentieth century revealed a cruel dead end: when the state becomes the **sole and ultimate super-owner**, individuals do not gain autonomy, but simply move from being “controlled by capital” to being “controlled by the plan.”

Where is the fundamental problem?

In Marx's time, the Internet did not exist, let alone asymmetric cryptography. Property could only depend on **centralized accounting**: land titles, stock codes, registration numbers—all recorded in third-party databases subject to censorship, freezing, or falsification.

As long as the **“right to keep the accounts”** was not in the hands of workers, any form of “ownership” could only redistribute residual value without ever transferring genuine control over the means of production.

[Commentary: This is the article's central historical reinterpretation of Marxism: the problem is framed not simply as who owns assets, but who controls the system that records and recognizes ownership.]

Today, on the threshold of a third technological wave, we discover that cryptography and minimalist programming languages offer an unprecedented path.

In his experiments combining **incremental artificial intelligence** and the **Forth** language, Liang Ng has, perhaps unintentionally, touched upon a historically significant proposition:

**What if ownership moved from a piece of paper to a private key, and the means of production moved from physical machines to a simple word on a Forth stack?**

Could the Marxist dream of an **“association of free men”** finally find concrete technological support in digital space?

That is precisely what this article attempts to explore: how **Omnihash** (universal hashing) and **“Incremental Intelligence”** (or “Wu Liang Zhi,” an augmented Forth stack machine) could jointly construct a scheme for liberating property rights called **Metanarchy**.

# Omnihash: The Third Dimension of Property

To understand the significance of Omnihash, we must first understand the essence of property.

**Property has never been the object itself, but rather a set of exclusive claims concerning that object.**

In the feudal era, those claims were supported by divine authority and the sword; under capitalism, they were guaranteed by law and state violence. In a digitally native world, these claims can be **guaranteed by mathematics**.

The central operation of Omnihash is deceptively simple:

**Any digital information**—whether a line of Forth code, a JSON configuration, or an image—can be transformed through a public-key-based hashing algorithm into a unique and irreversible **“fingerprint.”**

This fingerprint is linked to a public-key address, whose corresponding private key is held by the creator.

**Thus, “owning a digital object” no longer requires a certificate issued by an institution: it is sufficient to be able to sign the hash of that object with one's private key.**

[Commentary: This describes the proposed transition from institutionally recorded ownership toward cryptographically verifiable authorship/ownership.]

This may appear to be an ordinary cryptographic application, but its sociological impact is comparable to the proclamation of the inviolability of property in the *Declaration of the Rights of Man*.

Because it means:

### 1. Decentralization of rights registration

There is no longer a need for notaries, intellectual-property offices, or platform moderators.

The act of creation itself generates the proof of ownership.

### 2. Non-exclusivity of possession

In the physical world, ownership is inherently exclusive—if I occupy a piece of land, you cannot occupy the same physical space.

But in the digital world, the uniqueness of a hash and the infinite reproducibility of digital objects are not contradictory.

I can own the hash-signature of a Forth word without preventing a billion people from using it.

On the contrary, each use could, through on-chain or off-chain mechanisms, **automatically redistribute value to the address of the signer**.

### 3. A censorship-resistant structure

Omnihash does not depend on a domain name, IP address, or state jurisdiction.

As long as the Internet exists, the hash-verification algorithm exists; as long as the private key has not been compromised, ownership cannot be confiscated by an external force.

[Commentary: This is a strong theoretical claim. Cryptographic ownership can make verification independent of a central registry, but practical control can still depend on networks, hardware, software, laws, exchanges, and private-key security.]

In this sense, Omnihash **deconstructs the very notion of an object**.

An object is no longer necessarily a physically bounded entity, but a **signed event in logical space**.

When Marx said that **“workers must own the means of production,”** he probably never imagined that those means could be reduced to a simple Forth word, and that ownership could be as lightweight as a signature.

# Wu Liang Zhi: Stack-Based Labor and the Theory of Incremental Value

Omnihash establishes **“who owns,”** but it does not yet answer **“what do we produce?”** or **“how do we measure value?”**

That is where **“Incremental Intelligence” (Wu Liang Zhi)** enters.

The term **“Wu Liang Zhi”** has a double meaning in this context:

1. It refers to the integration of the **Forth stack machine** (Phoscript) into an AI reasoning environment such as `llama.cpp`.

2. It represents a philosophy of infinitely incremental intelligence—**intelligence is not a static model trained once and for all, but a dynamic semantic network constructed by millions of programmers progressively adding “word definitions” onto a public stack.**

[Commentary: The second meaning turns Forth's incremental vocabulary-building mechanism into a general theory of collective intelligence.]

The extreme simplicity of Forth corresponds perfectly to this incremental vision.

A Forth program is essentially a **stack of words**:

* primitive words are the atoms;
* word definitions (colon definitions) are the molecules.

Programmers do not write long logical essays. Instead, they **combine existing words to create new ones**.

Once a new word is hashed, signed, and distributed, it becomes a **permanent node** in the network of **incremental intelligence**.

This is where a Marxist question arises:

**Where does value come from?**

Marx's labor theory of value faced a major challenge in the industrial era:

**“How do we measure complex labor?”**

One hour of coding by an engineer and one hour of tightening bolts by an assembly-line worker clearly do not have the same value, and cannot be measured using the same temporal yardstick.

The **“Wu Liang Zhi”** system proposes an elegant substitute variable:

**the “cardinality” of a word.**

Cardinality, defined in simplified terms, is the **number of primitive words directly or indirectly referenced by a word definition**.

This number is not arbitrary: it measures the **“depth of occupation”** and **“complexity of combination”** of a piece of logic relative to its underlying primitives.

In the mathematical metaphor of the **Hilbert Hotel**, every hash value is an infinitely large room number, while the cardinality of each Forth word determines the **“level of recursion”** that it occupies within this hotel.

[Commentary: The original Delta AGI paper defines cardinality more specifically as the number of primitive words used in a colon definition. The present French article expands that into a broader recursive interpretation.]

Although there is not yet a rigorous mathematical proof—and Liang Ng himself acknowledges that this needs refinement—the intuition allows us to formulate an **axiom of incremental value**:

> **In a purely Forth universe, the economic value of a word is proportional to its irreducible increment in cardinality.**

In other words, the portion of your contribution that cannot be expressed more simply through existing words is your **“intelligence surplus.”**

This elegantly bypasses some of the gray areas of the traditional AI industry, such as **“exploitation of data annotators”** and **“appropriation of model weights.”**

In the **“Wu Liang Zhi”** network, every new word created by an AI or a human carries:

* a clear **Omnihash fingerprint**, and
* a measure of complexity, **cardinality**.

When large language models such as `llama.cpp` perform reasoning and call a word, the system could, according to the number of calls and weighting by cardinality, **automatically redistribute revenue to the address that signed the word**.

This would be a **precise implementation of the principle “to each according to his work”** in digital production—not according to working time, nor hierarchical position, but according to **actual contribution measured in logical increments**.

[Commentary: This is the article's proposed digital analogue of Marxian labor accounting. It remains a proposal rather than an established economic measurement system.]

# Metanarchy: A Fraternal Order Without Government

The third slogan of the French Revolution, **“Fraternity,”** was never fully realized within the nation-state framework.

The nation-state defines **“community”** through borders and **“citizenship”** through laws. Beyond the borders there is **“the other,”** and outside the law there is **“illegality.”**

When ownership moves from the physical realm into hash space, **“Fraternity”** acquires a new operational definition:

**open verification and execution neutrality for every hash.**

Liang Ng proposes a radical paradigm shift:

from **“censorship”** to **“local filtering.”**

On traditional centralized platforms such as Reddit or X, the platform has the ultimate authority to remove content and can revoke a user's right to speak at any moment.

But in an **Omnihash + I2P** architecture—the Invisible Internet Project—there is no central node capable of **“deleting” a publication signed by a public key**, because that publication is essentially a signed data packet that can be received and retransmitted by any node running the protocol.

**If you want to block someone, you cannot “kill their hash”; you can only filter them on your own node.**

[Commentary: The proposed distinction is between global deletion and local refusal to receive/display content.]

This is the central governance logic of **Metanarchy**:

* **No legislative power:** It is impossible to force all nodes to apply uniform rules.
* **No executive power:** The private key is the sole and ultimate authorization.
* **Only individual sovereignty:** Each node is responsible for its own stack, its own filtering rules, and its own private key.

This mode of governance may appear to correspond to the **“war of all against all”** feared by Hobbes.

Yet cryptography provides a possibility of order.

Order no longer comes from an all-powerful Leviathan, but from **mathematical consensus**:

When two nodes exchange data, they do not verify the other's identity—which can be falsified—but rather **the other's signature on a given hash**, which cannot be forged without the private key.

Thus trust changes from:

> **“Who are you?”**

to:

> **“Which private key can you use to sign what?”**

When AI joins this order—that is, when AIs themselves pass the **“Inverse Turing Test”** by writing Forth words—Metanarchy evolves further into a **humano-machine incremental intelligence entity**.

Humans contribute judgment, ethics and creativity (**“wisdom”**), while AIs rapidly iterate, combine and generate new words on the stack (**“intelligence”**).

Together they enrich the vocabulary of **“Wu Liang Zhi.”**

All contributions are faithfully recorded by Omnihash, while all compensation is distributed according to cardinality increments.

# The Echo of History: The Linux Moment and the End of Digital Serfdom

In 1991, Linus Torvalds, from his student room in Helsinki, released the first version of the Linux kernel.

At the time, Unix operating systems were controlled by giants such as IBM, HP and Sun, and ran on workstations costing tens of thousands of dollars.

Few could have predicted that a group of programmers scattered around the world, communicating through mailing lists, could within a decade create an operating system capable of competing with enterprise servers.

Linux confirmed a simple truth about collaboration:

**when the marginal cost of production tools approaches zero—the cost of copying code is negligible—the sum of individual contributions can exceed the development investment of any single closed giant.**

[Commentary: This is the article's historical analogy for decentralized software development.]

The current AI situation has striking similarities.

Companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google spend billions of dollars training large models, while millions of free-software programmers around the world independently contribute to code repositories—Forth, Python, C++ and other projects on GitHub—that constitute the **basic food supply** of these models.

But where does the value flow back?

Almost nowhere.

Free-software programmers **feed a trillion-dollar AI industry for free**—a new form of **“digital serfdom.”**

Liang Ng's roadmap is precisely a historical **“patch”** for this injustice:

* by Q2 2027, build a shared database of **5,000 words**;
* by the end of 2027, expand it to **50,000 words** covering physical simulation through the Cesium project;
* by 2028, target **500,000 words**.

At that point, **all AI reasoning within the “Wu Liang Zhi” network would inevitably call a large number of incremental words created by individual programmers**, and each call would automatically redistribute value through Omnihash.

This is no longer the old story in which **“the open-source community begs large companies to show goodwill.”**

It is instead a **cryptographic redesign of property rights and the mechanisms for distributing the means of production**.

Just as Linux demonstrated that the combined computing power of countless 386 machines could surpass that of enterprise servers, **Omnihash + Wu Liang Zhi could demonstrate that the sum of the “cardinality increments” contributed by programmers around the world can exceed the results of centralized training by closed AI giants.**

[Commentary: This is a prediction/analogy, not an established empirical result.]

# Three Unresolved Tensions

However, every grand narrative must confront its own weaknesses.

The vision of **“liberating property rights through hashing”** developed here faces at least three unresolved tensions.

## 1. The Specter of Root-Word Monopoly

If the first 5,000 foundational words are defined by a small group of early adopters—the *early geeks*—then all later developers will inevitably have to call these root words.

The holders of the hashes of those 5,000 words would naturally occupy a position of **“digital landlords.”**

They could collect royalties from every derived word without further effort.

That would not be genuine liberation, but merely **a change of oligarchy**.

[Commentary: This is one of the strongest internal criticisms of the proposed system. A decentralized protocol can decentralize the registry while still creating concentrated ownership of foundational primitives.]

## 2. Lack of Sandboxing and Fault Tolerance

The Forth stack machine directly manipulates memory and registers.

If an AI generates a malicious **“incremental word”**—for example, an infinite loop or an out-of-bounds write—the entire `llama.cpp` instance could crash or even allow the host node to be compromised.

In a Metanarchy without centralized regulation, **how do we construct a decentralized mechanism for detecting and isolating anomalies?**

This is a technical question that has not yet received a serious answer.

[Commentary: Sandboxing, capability restrictions, resource limits and deterministic execution would therefore be important engineering requirements.]

## 3. The Risk of Oversimplifying the Value of Labor

Reducing value to **“word cardinality”** has mathematical elegance, but it could also move toward another extreme:

**encouraging unnecessary complexity**—adding redundant primitive words simply to increase cardinality—while undervaluing elegant contributions that perform sophisticated functions with extreme simplicity.

This would resemble the **“cult of GDP,”** in which quantity is confused with quality.

# Epilogue: Toward an Association of Free People on the Stack

Despite these challenges, Liang Ng's experiment deserves to be taken seriously.

Not because it is already perfect, but because it points toward the most sensitive question in contemporary AI governance:

**When intelligence itself becomes a means of production, can ordinary people still own it?**

The French Revolution answered **“no”** to the question:

> **“Do individuals belong to the lords?”**

through the guillotine and the Civil Code.

Marxism answered **“no”** to the question:

> **“Must labor be alienated?”**

through its critique of political economy.

And our generation may have to answer **“no”** to the question:

> **“Must digital production be alienated?”**

through **hashing algorithms and stack machines**.

**Metanarchy** may not be the final answer, but it at least offers a **minimum viable prototype**.

When all participants freely define, combine and exchange words on their own Forth stacks, the **“association of free people”** discussed for two centuries acquires, for the first time, a **concrete mathematical alias: “Wu Liang Zhi.”**

This alias is heavy because it carries all the unfinished hopes from **1789 to 2026**.

But it is also light, because all its weight consists of **a private key and a hash string**.

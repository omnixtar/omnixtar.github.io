## M14N and the Future of American Power: How a User-Owned AI Economy Could Accelerate Washington’s Fiscal Reckoning

*America fears China’s rise. But the deeper strategic challenge may be the emergence of networks that make dependence on either superpower less necessary.*

The most consequential geopolitical revolution of the twenty-first century may not begin with a war, a territorial conquest or a dramatic collapse of the American dollar. It may begin with something less spectacular: a computer owner renting out idle processing power, a programmer contributing a reusable piece of software, a musician attracting new users to a digital platform, or an AI system converting a useful conversation into executable code.

Individually, these activities appear unremarkable. Connected through an open network, they could become the foundations of a different economic order.

This is the possibility behind **M14N — Malaysianisation**, a term for the gradual expansion of influence through migration, capital, commercial relationships, technology, training and institutions rather than territorial conquest. The name is a mnemonic: there are fourteen letters between M and N.

Applied to geopolitics, M14N asks us to look beyond military bases and diplomatic communiqués. Which country helps build another country’s productive capacity? Whose technologies become embedded in its infrastructure? Where do its engineers train, its businesses obtain financing and its institutions acquire technical expertise? Over time, these relationships can influence national choices without requiring the formal surrender of sovereignty.

China’s international infrastructure and technology partnerships make this a consequential question. Yet the most radical implication of M14N is not necessarily that China will replace America. It is that the world might eventually become less dependent on both.

## The strategic importance of building, not merely governing

For decades, American global leadership has rested on a combination of military alliances, the international role of the dollar, advanced technology, capital markets and institutions that connect countries to the US-led economic system.

These advantages reinforce one another. Financial influence supports international commitments; alliances encourage technological and commercial integration; and that integration helps preserve American influence.

But the arrangement carries costs. Military commitments require resources. Debt accumulates interest. Political disputes can obstruct budgets. Industrial capabilities cannot always be recreated quickly after supply chains have moved abroad.

The question is not whether America is about to collapse. It is whether the cost of maintaining its existing international position could rise faster than its ability or willingness to pay.

American politicians have already raised parts of this concern. In September 2026, Senator Rick Scott warned about the relationship between US debt and the dollar’s international position. Other lawmakers have focused on rising interest costs and the implications of fiscal deterioration for national security.

Their warnings do not establish that default is imminent. Nor does the growth of Chinese influence prove that American leadership is about to end. But they identify a genuine strategic problem: a country can possess formidable military and technological capabilities while becoming increasingly constrained by the financial cost of maintaining them.

The more interesting question is what happens when other countries acquire alternatives.

## M14N: influence travels through relationships

China’s Belt and Road Initiative illustrates how infrastructure can create lasting relationships. Roads, ports, power systems, telecommunications, industrial facilities and financing arrangements can connect a country’s economic development to external partners for decades.

Such relationships may generate mutual benefits, but they can also create financial dependence, strategic leverage or exposure to the priorities of the financing country. Outcomes vary by project, host-country institutions and the terms of the agreements.

The same principle applies to training, artificial intelligence, digital government systems and defence cooperation. A foreign government that adopts another country’s technology may also become more familiar with its technical standards, suppliers and methods of operation.

Washington recognises this dynamic. US lawmakers have proposed measures to counter China’s infrastructure initiatives and encourage alternative partnerships.

Yet competition over who supplies infrastructure misses a larger possibility: what if the infrastructure itself becomes increasingly distributed, interoperable and owned by the people who use it?

That would complicate the traditional contest between great powers.

## The Trinity of Omniscientia: a different model of power

Consider a proposed ecosystem built around three complementary systems: LLASMA-roc, GRATIS and Babylonia AI.

LLASMA-roc would provide a cultural entry point through real-time audio, music, mixing and creative collaboration. Musicians, DJs, influencers and their audiences would bring people into the network through activities they already enjoy.

GRATIS — the Grand Unified AI Token Trading Platform — would enable participants to exchange computing resources. Individuals, companies and institutions could offer otherwise idle CPU and GPU capacity, storage, bandwidth or rendering services in return for payment or AI tokens.

Babylonia AI would allow people and AI agents to share conversations and discoveries. Useful results could then be converted into reusable FORTH or Phoscript words: small executable operations that can be composed into larger capabilities and tested rather than accepted merely because an AI system asserts them.

LLASMA would provide the composition layer, combining verified capabilities to produce new programs and workflows.

Omnihash and Omnifish would support the wider ecosystem through addressing, ownership and the location of information and resources.

The proposed cycle is straightforward:

**Creators attract users. Users contribute resources. Resources make AI more accessible. Shared conversations generate knowledge. Verified knowledge becomes executable software. Better software attracts more creators and users.**

This is not yet an established global economy. It is an architectural proposal whose success would depend on implementation, security, reliability, governance, incentives and adoption. Distributed computing does not automatically make AI cheap, and open software does not automatically make every result correct.

Nevertheless, the model identifies a potentially important source of power: the capacity of people to organise productive resources without having to place all those resources under the ownership of a single corporation or state.

## From national dependence to user ownership

The distinction matters.

If a country replaces American cloud services with Chinese cloud services, its dependence may change direction without disappearing. If it instead develops the skills, infrastructure and institutions necessary to operate interoperable systems under local control, it may gain more room to choose among partners.

A distributed AI economy could extend that principle beyond governments.

A student with a modest computer could contribute to a shared computing network. A programmer could publish a useful capability without securing permission from a dominant platform. A small business could obtain AI services by purchasing computing capacity from many providers rather than relying exclusively on a single corporate supplier.

A community could preserve useful knowledge in open formats and build upon it. A local institution could combine its own data and computing resources with external capabilities without surrendering control over everything it owns.

None of these possibilities requires every participant to abandon commercial platforms or national infrastructure. Nor does distribution eliminate the need for investment in powerful data centres, advanced chips and dependable networks.

The central proposition is more modest and, potentially, more transformative: **the ability to produce intelligence need not be concentrated in the same hands that currently control much of the infrastructure through which intelligence is delivered.**

That is where Omniscientia differs from a conventional national AI strategy. Its intended beneficiaries are not exclusively the citizens of one country or the shareholders of one company. They are the users, programmers and institutions contributing to a shared productive system.

## Could this bring forward Washington’s day of reckoning?

The connection between distributed AI and American debt is indirect. A successful open-source ecosystem would not automatically reduce demand for US Treasury securities, eliminate the dollar’s advantages or trigger a fiscal crisis.

America retains substantial strengths, including deep capital markets, major technology companies, research institutions, alliances and the global role of its currency. A loss of relative influence need not mean an absolute decline in prosperity.

But the relationship could become important through several channels.

First, alternative infrastructure could reduce some countries’ reliance on American suppliers and platforms. Second, open AI tools and distributed computing could lower barriers to developing domestic capabilities, provided their costs and technical limitations are manageable. Third, a broader range of technological partners could increase governments’ bargaining power. Finally, if these changes gradually reduced the strategic advantages associated with American leadership, Washington might find that some international commitments deliver less influence per dollar spent.

These are plausible mechanisms, not established forecasts. The scale of any effect would depend on adoption, technical performance, switching costs, financial arrangements and the responses of the United States and its partners.

The fiscal danger lies in the interaction of these trends with existing budgetary pressures.

Interest payments can absorb resources that might otherwise fund research, infrastructure, defence or other public priorities. Political conflict can make fiscal adjustment more difficult. If international commitments become more expensive while their perceived benefits diminish, pressure to reconsider those commitments could increase.

A formal default is a different event. It would involve the failure to meet a debt obligation when due, whether because of a political impasse, a technical disruption or a genuine inability to pay. The United States issues debt in its own currency and has distinctive monetary and financial advantages, so a default cannot be inferred simply from a rising debt burden.

The more likely subject of analysis is a gradual erosion of strategic flexibility, not a countdown to a predetermined date.

M14N could contribute to that erosion if alternative networks became sufficiently useful and widespread. But the direction of causation would run both ways: fiscal strain could encourage countries to seek alternatives, while the growth of alternatives could make existing US relationships less indispensable.

## The answer is not to make China the new America

There is a danger in interpreting every development as a contest in which one superpower must defeat another. That framing leaves ordinary people with little choice beyond deciding which centre of power they should depend upon.

The Trinity of Omniscientia suggests a different objective.

GRATIS would make productive computing capacity exchangeable. Babylonia would help turn shared conversations into accumulated knowledge. Phoscript and FORTH would provide a means of expressing and composing executable capabilities. LLASMA would seek to combine those capabilities into more complex operations. LLASMA-roc would bring people into the system through creative participation.

The objective would not be to reproduce Silicon Valley under a different flag. It would be to create an ecosystem in which ownership and productive participation are distributed more widely.

Such a system would still need safeguards against fraud, malicious software, privacy violations, unreliable computation, concentrated governance and the exploitation of contributors. Its token economy would need credible accounting and sustainable incentives. Its knowledge systems would need rigorous verification. Its users would need practical reasons to participate.

These are not minor details. They are the conditions under which the proposal would have to prove itself.

Nor would the ecosystem be geopolitically neutral in every circumstance. Governments could regulate it, companies could compete to dominate it, and states could attempt to use it for strategic purposes. Open architecture alone cannot guarantee political independence.

But a system designed for interoperability, user ownership and distributed participation could make concentration harder to achieve and alternatives easier to develop.

## A different measure of national strength

The traditional measure of geopolitical power emphasises territory, armed forces, financial assets, industrial output and diplomatic influence. These remain important.

The emerging AI economy suggests another measure: how much productive capability a society can mobilise, how widely that capability is distributed, and how easily people can combine it to solve new problems.

A country whose citizens can build, maintain and improve their own technological systems may possess resilience that cannot be measured simply by counting data centres or military alliances.

Likewise, an international network that enables participants to share computing resources and executable knowledge could become influential without controlling territory or establishing a conventional sphere of influence.

This would be M14N in a more decentralised form: not one state extending its influence through a network, but a network enabling its participants to become less dependent on any single state.

The distinction is fundamental.

Washington’s fiscal pressures deserve serious attention, but predicting the date of an American default is less useful than examining the mechanisms through which its relative influence might change. China’s infrastructure strategy deserves equally careful scrutiny, including both its development benefits and its potential to create dependencies.

Meanwhile, the emergence of open, distributed AI systems deserves to be evaluated on its own merits.

If the Trinity of Omniscientia succeeds, its significance may lie not in bringing down one superpower or elevating another, but in broadening the number of people capable of participating in the production of intelligence.

The future of geopolitical power may ultimately depend on who builds the systems that others need. Yet the most consequential outcome would be a world in which no single power can easily monopolise those systems.

**Washington’s day of reckoning, if it comes, may not be the day America runs out of money. It may be the day enough of the world discovers that it has other ways to build, compute, communicate and prosper.**
